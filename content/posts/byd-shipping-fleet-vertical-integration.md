The image that made news was a car carrier with 7,000 vehicle slots sailing out of Yantai Port in January 2024. The framing that followed was simple and mostly wrong: BYD bought a ship.

It did not. The *BYD Explorer No.1* was built by CIMC Raffles for the shipping company Zodiac Maritime and **chartered** to BYD, with the option to carry other cargo. BYD announced a seven-vessel plan alongside it, but announced plans are not hulls. The more revealing fact is what happened over the following two and a half years, and what it says about a company that does not want to be a car manufacturer that also happens to ship cars.

By September 2025 BYD had eight car carriers in operation. By September 2026 it reportedly had ten more on order. The thesis worth examining is not that one automaker bought a boat. It is that a vertically integrated manufacturer facing a margin collapse at home concluded that the binding constraint on its export strategy was not factories or batteries — it was floating steel.

---

## The Margin Problem That Forced the Decision

BYD's 2026 numbers describe a company in two places at once. Chinese vehicle exports reached 1,127,000 units from January through August 2026, up 88% year-on-year. Over the same period, domestic sales under the BYD brand fell 43% to 1.15 million units.

The gap is the strategy. China's automotive sector profit margin fell to 3.2% in the first quarter of 2026 according to the China Passenger Car Association. In August 2026, 78% of Chinese passenger car sales were domestic brands, up from roughly 35% in 2020. Overseas, an EV that sells for a premium in Europe, Thailand, or Brazil is a margin refuge, not a growth market.

That reframes the fleet decision. A company shipping 184,000 vehicles a month out of China is not primarily buying freight capacity. It is defending the margin differential between what it earns at home and what it earns abroad — and freight is a large, variable, and largely uncontrollable component of that differential.

## The Capacity Wall

The specific constraint was pure car and truck carrier (PCTC) availability. Vehicle shipping is a specialized market with a thin supply of purpose-built hulls, most of them owned by a small number of Japanese and European operators. When Chinese EV exports accelerated, they collided with a global shortage of exactly the right kind of ship.

Charter rates for PCTCs reportedly surged to around $90,000 per day in 2026, against a market that had been structurally soft before the export boom. For a shipper renting a vessel year to year, that is a margin line moving independently of anything management controls.

The response across Chinese manufacturing was convergent. Anhui Provincial Port and Shipping Group formed a joint venture with Chery and JAC, ordering three 7,000-CEU LNG dual-fuel carriers from Wuhu Shipyard. SAIC moved MG vehicles through its own logistics subsidiary, Anji Logistics, which operates both owned ro-ro capacity and third-party services. BYD took the most direct route and built the largest fleet fastest.

This is where BYD's own scale became the advantage. Battery technology was already in-house. A vessel is a steel hull with a battery-backed energy storage system and a shaft generator — and BYD sells both the storage system and the drivetrain, so it can spec a ship around components it manufactures at automotive volumes.

## The Tariff Geography

The second driver is where the ships go, and increasingly where they load from.

The European Union concluded its anti-subsidy investigation into Chinese EVs in October 2024, applying an additional tariff of 17% to BYD on top of the standard 10% car import duty. Shipping a vehicle from a Chinese port into Europe is now a more expensive proposition, and no amount of fleet ownership changes the duty.

Fleet ownership changes everything around it. Ships from BYD's Thailand plant are not subject to those countervailing tariffs. In September 2025 the *BYD Zhengzhou* carried right-hand-drive vehicles from that facility to the United Kingdom — BYD's first vehicle exports from outside China. The *Explorer No.1*'s Brazil call and the 9,200-CEU *BYD Shenzhen* serve a similar function: moving vehicles to markets where the tariff exposure differs.

By owning hulls, BYD can also choose to load locally. That is a materially different logistics architecture from a Chinese automaker that contracts freight from Shenzhen and accepts whatever route and terminal a carrier offers.

## Why Chartered, Not Owned

The Explorer No.1 arrangement deserves attention because it is more common than the "BYD owns ships" narrative suggests. BYD also signed a memorandum of understanding with Hyundai Glovis in October 2024 to share deep-ocean vessel capacity for China-to-export shipments.

Capacity sharing between manufacturers is rational when utilization is the problem rather than access to the market. A vessel that runs a fixed route carrying only one manufacturer's cars may underperform one chartered to a pool of cargo customers, particularly in the early phase when export volumes are still ramping and load factors are unpredictable. A charter converts a fixed capital commitment into a variable one and transfers the risk of an empty sailing.

The logical progression is visible in the vessel mix: charter first to validate the route and volume, then ownership once the demand is demonstrated. BYD reported eight vessels in service by September 2025, and the September 2026 report of ten more 9,200-CEU vessels on order for 2027–2029 delivery — a fleet of 18 with combined capacity above 130,000 CEU — is consistent with a company that has moved past the question of whether it needs ships and is now deciding how many to build.

That report deserves a caveat. It comes from New Ships and Robin Assafina, and carries no official announcement from BYD or China Merchants Industry. The capacity-to-volume translation also needs care: 130,000 CEU is carrying capacity, not annual throughput. CarNewsChina's estimate of 2.5 million vehicles per year of export capability is an inference, not a company figure.

## The Precedent

Automaker-owned shipping is not new. Japanese manufacturers scaled global exports with owned or long-term chartered fleets through their trading houses — Toyota's long relationship with NYK being the most cited example. What is different is the compression: BYD assembled eight ships in under two years and ordered ten more in a single reported decision, a rate that reflects Chinese shipyard capacity and state-backed financing rather than a genuinely novel idea.

## What the Fleet Buys Operationally

Ownership changes more than the charter line, though the accounting is the part most readily quantified. A charter is a rate negotiated per voyage or per period against a market that moves weekly. A ship is a schedule the company sets.

That control shows up in three places. The first is load factor. A chartered vessel sailing for one automaker must be filled by that automaker, so any shortfall in export volume against forecast is absorbed entirely by BYD. An owner can commit a portion of capacity to its own trade and return the rest to the wider charter market, as the *Explorer No.1* arrangement allows. A vessel in a pool earns across several cargoes rather than depending on one.

The second is routing. With freight bought, the carrier's network determines which ports are served, when, and how often. With hulls, a manufacturer can add a call at a load port that matters to it — Yantai for domestic production, Xiamen for the export terminal — without negotiating a service change. The Europe calls in particular are exposed to congestion and to Red Sea routing decisions that a cargo owner inherits but does not control.

The third is the unit economics of a purpose-built ship. A PCTC designed for electric vehicles differs from a general ro-ro: battery mass changes weight distribution, and rolling stock requires higher deck strength, different ventilation, and specific safety arrangements for thermally sensitive cargo. Vessels designed with EVs in mind are now entering the market, and an owner is better positioned than a charterer to specify those requirements at the yard. An owner also retains the residual value of a well-maintained PCTC and can charter it out during export troughs, which is a genuine hedge — the same asset earns freight revenue on any leg, not only on BYD cars.

None of this makes the fleet cheap. It makes the cost more knowable.

## The Sizing Question

Eight ships in service and ten more reported on order is a decision about how much of the export business should be owned rather than rented. The arithmetic is worth setting out, because the figure that circulates — 2.5 million vehicles a year — is a derived estimate and much weaker than it appears.

The distinction is between carrying capacity and annual throughput. 130,000 CEU across eighteen vessels is how much can be aboard at once. Converting that into vehicles per year requires assumptions about round-trip duration, port time, sailing frequency, and load factor, and CEU is a volumetric measure that does not map cleanly onto a fixed number of cars of any size. A vessel held at European discharge for three weeks of loading and unloading is not carrying anything.

A single 7,000-CEU vessel on a China–Europe round trip is conventionally estimated at roughly a dozen to fifteen sailings a year, depending heavily on schedule reliability. Applied across the fleet, that range brackets the 1 million figure attributed to the original eight ships — but "roughly" is doing real work, and Red Sea diversions have historically added days per round trip that reduce that count materially.

So the defensible claim is directional: the original fleet is sized to move on the order of a million vehicles a year, and the reported order is intended to roughly two and a half times that capability. The precise figure matters less than the fact that BYD is committing to two and a half times the hulls — which is only rational if the export outlook is expected to hold for the better part of a decade, given deliveries running to 2029.

The fleet is effectively a bet placed on export demand remaining large for long enough to amortize steel.

## The Risk Analysts Keep Naming

That bet runs against a known trend. Matthias Schmidt of Schmidt Automotive Research expects ro-ro costs to fall sharply as newbuild capacity arrives, noting that some vessels now entering the market are designed specifically for EVs. The ships Chinese OEMs chose to wait for are on order and being delivered through 2027 and 2029.

If charter rates normalize while BYD's fleet is already built, the company is left holding depreciating steel into a softer market. That is the standard risk of vertical integration, and it is worth naming plainly: owning ships converts a variable freight expense into a fixed capital burden, and it is only a good trade while demand reliably exceeds charter supply.

The domestic price war is the second risk. BYD's own export thesis depends on overseas margins staying attractive long enough for 18 vessels to earn their keep. A sustained global move toward domestic Chinese brands' cost parity would compress the very differential the fleet was built to protect.

## What This Actually Signals

The fleet is usually described as a logistics decision. The evidence suggests it is better read as a manufacturing decision that happens to have a shipping component.

BYD's vertical integration is usually discussed in terms of batteries and semiconductors, where owning the stack is defensible because the technology is where the margin lives. A car carrier is closer to the opposite: a commodity asset bought off a production line, with no technology advantage.

That makes it a statement about constraint rather than ambition. The reason a battery maker went into shipping is that it ran out of a different kind of supply — not cells, but hulls, and the availability to load them where tariffs permit. Once that constraint became the bottleneck on the export strategy that the domestic market's collapse made existential, the answer was to own the bottleneck.

The most useful lesson is narrow. Vertical integration is not a virtue in itself; it is a response to a specific dependency that has tightened. Companies that integrate broadly by default tend to accumulate capital in whatever is fashionable. The companies that integrate well are reacting to a constraint they have actually measured. BYD's fleet is a fairly precise answer to a fairly specific problem — and the more interesting question now is what happens when the newbuilds land and the constraint it was built to solve disappears.
