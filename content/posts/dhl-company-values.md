# DHL Company Values: The Respect & Results Framework Behind the World's Leading Logistics Company

DHL Group moves packages across **220+ countries and territories**, employs roughly **584,000 people**, and generated about **€83 billion in revenue in 2025**. At that scale, the word "values" usually signals corporate wallpaper — aspirational adjectives that nobody can enforce across half a million employees. DHL's framework is different. It is built around two words — **"Respect & Results"** — and it is unusually specific: ten behavioral commitments that are written as actions, not abstractions.

This article synthesizes DHL Group's publicly documented strategy, corporate history, and official communications to examine what the company's values actually are, where they came from, and how they are wired into the operating model of the world's largest logistics group.

---

## From Three Initials to a Global Group

The DHL story begins in 1969, when Adrian Dalsey, Larry Hillblom, and Robert Lynn founded an air-express service in San Francisco. The company name is literally the founders' initials — an origin story that explains a great deal about the culture: pragmatic, fast, and built by operators rather than strategists.

The founding team reportedly began by carrying shipping documents on commercial flights between San Francisco and Honolulu, allowing cargo to clear customs before the ship arrived. That "shipment before the shipment" insight became the seed of the express industry.

DHL Group, however, is a much older institution wearing a modern brand. The group traces **more than 500 years of postal history**, from the founding of the modern postal system by Franz von Taxis in 1490 through Deutsche Bundespost, Deutsche Post, and eventually the acquisition of DHL in 2003. Key milestones in the group's modern era:

| Year | Milestone |
|------|-----------|
| 1969 | DHL founded in San Francisco (Dalsey, Hillblom, Lynn) |
| 1979 | DHL expands its network internationally |
| 1989 | DHL enters China |
| 2000 | Deutsche Post acquires Danzas and AEI |
| 2003 | Deutsche Post acquires DHL |
| 2004 | Unified DHL brand for the worldwide express and logistics business |

Today the group operates five divisions — **Express, Global Forwarding, Supply Chain, eCommerce, and Post & Parcel Germany** — under two brands: DHL (parcel, express, freight, supply chain, e-commerce logistics) and Deutsche Post (the largest postal provider in Europe).

## The Value Architecture: A "Strong Foundation"

DHL Group's own framing of corporate culture is worth quoting directly: while strategy changes, certain elements "remain constant, deeply rooted in our understanding of who we are and how we operate." The company calls this its **strong foundation**, and it has four layers:

| Layer | Content |
|-------|---------|
| **Purpose** | "Connecting people, improving lives" (adopted 2011) |
| **Values** | "Respect & Results" — ten behavioral commitments |
| **Customer promise** | "DHL is a quality provider – we deliver against what we promise" |
| **Bottom lines** | Employer, Provider, Investment, and Green Logistics of Choice |

The logic of the stack is deliberate: purpose explains **why** the company exists, values govern **how** people act, the customer promise defines the **standard** of delivery, and the bottom lines translate all of it into measurable corporate goals. Values in this architecture are not decoration — they are the governance layer between identity and execution.

## Respect: The Five Commitments

The first half of the motto — **Respect** — is defined by five commitments, all phrased as first-person plural actions:

| # | Commitment |
|---|------------|
| 1 | We prioritize the health and safety of our employees |
| 2 | We value diversity and trust |
| 3 | We care for and appreciate each other and address negative behaviors |
| 4 | We promote open and honest feedback |
| 5 | We aim to inspire – our colleagues, our customers, and our partners – every day and everywhere |

Several design choices stand out. First, **safety leads the list** — for a company operating aircraft, warehouses, and road fleets, physical safety is not a slogan but an operational metric. Second, the commitments pair positive duties ("care for and appreciate") with corrective duties ("address negative behaviors"), which makes the value actionable: respect means intervening when behavior falls short, not just feeling positive. Third, the feedback commitment institutionalizes upward and lateral communication — a structural necessity in a 584,000-person organization where silent problems propagate.

## Results: The Five Commitments

The second half — **Results** — is defined by another five commitments:

| # | Commitment |
|---|------------|
| 1 | We clearly communicate expectations and achieve our goals with focus and drive |
| 2 | We take responsibility for our actions and decisions |
| 3 | We never compromise on integrity and compliance with the law |
| 4 | We are agile and seize opportunities |
| 5 | We help each other to succeed and be successful |

The pairing is the framework's core insight: **respect without results is sentiment; results without respect is exploitation.** DHL's public positioning treats the two as mutually reinforcing — a "Great Company to Work for All" is one where care and performance coexist. Notably, the results list also contains a hard constraint: "never compromise on integrity and compliance with the law." The value system explicitly subordinates performance to legality, which matters for a group that moves goods across borders and is subject to customs, trade, and sanctions regimes in hundreds of jurisdictions.

## How Values Become Operations

Values only matter if they change decisions. DHL Group's framework connects the "Respect & Results" commitments to four measurable corporate bottom lines:

- **Employer of Choice** — safe and healthy workplace, "Safety First" culture, employee well-being, and the Respect & Results values themselves
- **Provider of Choice** — best-in-class customer experience through customer-centricity, digital innovation, and quality
- **Investment of Choice** — profitable, sustainable growth with reliable execution, compliance, and cybersecurity
- **Green Logistics of Choice** — the fourth bottom line, added in Strategy 2030, making low-carbon logistics a differentiating feature

The values are also codified into binding documents. The DHL Group **Code of Conduct** translates the value framework into compliance standards binding on all colleagues, and the group's **Supplier Code of Conduct** extends the expectations to the supply chain. This matters: a value that exists only in a mission statement is a hope; a value referenced in a code of conduct is a contract.

Tangible examples connect the values to operations:

- **GoGreen (2009)** — the group's climate protection program, predating most industry net-zero commitments and evolving into the 2050 net-zero pledge made in 2018
- **GARD (2012)** — the "GoHelp" disaster management program, deploying logistics expertise after natural disasters, an operational expression of the purpose ("connecting people, improving lives")
- **Leipzig/Halle hub (2009)** — one of the world's largest air cargo hubs, an expression of the Results value of seizing opportunities with focus
- **DHL Innovation Center (2008)** — a public demonstration facility, institutionalizing the "agile and seize opportunities" commitment
- **Strategy 2030 (2025)** — the current growth strategy, "Accelerate sustainable growth," which explicitly keeps the values framework as its constant foundation while adding the fourth bottom line

## What This Teaches Us

Read as a case study, DHL's values framework offers five transferable principles:

**1. Values should be behavioral, not aspirational.**
"Respect" and "Results" are single words, but each is defined by five concrete, checkable actions ("we address negative behaviors," "we take responsibility"). A value that cannot be translated into observable behavior cannot be managed.

**2. Care and performance are designed as complements, not trade-offs.**
The framework refuses the common either/or — human treatment versus output. Its explicit claim is that a great company requires both, and that respect enables results over the long term.

**3. Values are anchored in strategy, not bolted on.**
The values sit inside the "strong foundation" that persists across strategy cycles. Strategy changes (Strategy 2015 → Strategy 2030); the foundation does not. This gives employees stability of identity while the business pivots.

**4. Values scale through documents and metrics.**
Half a million employees cannot absorb values through osmosis. The group codifies them in the Code of Conduct, operationalizes them in the bottom lines, and measures them through "Employer of Choice" type targets.

**5. Sustainability became a value, not a campaign.**
Elevating "Green Logistics of Choice" to a fourth bottom line (Strategy 2030) signals that decarbonization is a strategic commitment with capital behind it, consistent with the 2050 net-zero pledge made in 2018.

## Forward Outlook

Under Strategy 2030 — "Accelerate sustainable growth" — the values framework remains the constant foundation while the group pushes on three fronts: growth in its five divisions, modernization of the group structure, and net-zero greenhouse gas emissions aligned with defined targets. Recent moves (the J.F. Hillebrand Group acquisition in 2024, the CRYOPDP acquisition and new Dubai Innovation Center in 2025) indicate that the "Results" half of the motto — agility and seizing opportunities — is being applied to high-margin logistics niches and pharma/cold-chain specialization.

The open question for any observer is whether the "Respect" half scales as the group grows: whether safety, diversity, and feedback commitments hold up across acquisitions, outsourced operations, and a labor market where logistics work is under constant pressure. That, ultimately, is the test of whether a value system is real — not how it reads in an annual report, but how it survives contact with the operations.
